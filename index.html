How to check a long-distance mover in 5 minutes

Moves that cross a state line are regulated by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA), not by Colorado. Moves that start and end inside Colorado fall under the Colorado Public Utilities Commission instead. For a move from Denver to, say, Phoenix or Dallas, you want the federal checks:

  1. Get the USDOT number. Every interstate household-goods mover must have one and show it in its ads and on its trucks. If a company won't give it to you, stop there.
  2. Look it up. Enter the number at FMCSA's Protect Your Move search or the SAFER Company Snapshot. Look for active operating authority for household goods, the company's legal name and address, and whether it's listed as a carrier, a broker, or both.
  3. Carrier or broker? A carrier owns the trucks. A broker sells the move and hands it to a carrier. Brokers are legal when registered and upfront about it, but ask which carrier will actually load your things and check that company too.
  4. Match the names. The name on the estimate, the contract (bill of lading) and the truck should match the FMCSA record. A different name on moving day is a red flag.
  5. Read the complaint history. FMCSA publishes household-goods complaint data, and the Better Business Bureau shows complaints and how the company answered them. A few complaints are normal for a busy mover; unanswered ones are not.

The mover must also give you FMCSA's Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move information and tell you how its dispute-settlement (arbitration) program works.

Reading an interstate moving estimate

Long-distance moves are usually priced by the weight of your shipment and the distance, using the mover's published tariff, plus charges for extra services. Federal rules allow two kinds of written estimate, and the difference matters most on delivery day.

Extra charges that change the total

  • Shuttle: if a full-size truck can't reach your door (narrow streets, steep mountain driveways, some downtown buildings), goods move to a smaller truck for a fee.
  • Long carry and stairs: distance from the truck to your door, and flights without an elevator.
  • Packing and materials: full packing can add a lot; you can pack yourself and leave fragile items to the crew.
  • Storage-in-transit: if your new place isn't ready, short-term storage and an extra delivery are billed separately.

Coverage for your belongings

Interstate movers must offer Full Value Protection: they repair, replace, or pay current value for what they lose or damage, and may charge for it. The alternative, released value, costs nothing extra but only covers 60 cents per pound per article, so a 10-pound TV is worth about $6 in a claim. You only get released value if you sign a waiver, so read before you sign.

Leaving Denver: timing tips movers will thank you for

  • Going west in winter? Colorado's chain law for commercial vehicles applies on I-70 between Dotsero and Morrison from September 1 to May 31, and storms close the mountain passes. Build a buffer day into delivery windows for moves toward Utah, Nevada or California, and ask the mover which route the truck will take.
  • Summer and month-end fill up first. Most households move between late spring and early fall, and around the first and last days of the month. If your dates are flexible, a mid-month weekday is easier to book.
  • Book the building, not just the truck. Apartments and condos often require an elevator reservation, a certificate of insurance from the mover, or set move-out hours. Ask your building early and pass the details to the mover.
  • Get the survey done. A mover should see what you're moving, in person or by video, before giving you a price. A firm price over the phone for a whole house, sight unseen, is one of the oldest warning signs.

Red flags that should end the conversation

  • A large cash deposit before the move.
  • No USDOT number, or one that doesn't match the company name.
  • A quote far below the others with no survey.
  • Blank or unsigned paperwork, or being asked to sign before the inventory is written up.
  • A truck that arrives with a different company's name and no explanation.
  • Threats to hold your belongings until you pay more than the binding price, or more than 110% of a non-binding estimate. Report that to FMCSA's National Consumer Complaint Database at 1-888-368-7238.

Questions people ask

Is this a moving company?

No. It's a free referral service run by Eye To Ad Media in Denver. We pass your request to up to three interstate movers whose FMCSA registration we've checked. The mover you choose does the move and gives you the contract.

Do you only help with moves out of Colorado?

This site is for long-distance, state-to-state moves that start or end in the Denver area. For a move within Colorado, look for a mover with a Colorado PUC household goods permit; the PUC lists permitted movers on its website.

Can a mover charge more than the estimate?

With a non-binding estimate the final price can be higher, but the mover can't require more than 110% of the estimate at delivery; any remaining amount is billed later. With a binding estimate the price is fixed for what's listed, unless you add items or services.

What does "60 cents per pound" coverage mean?

It's released value protection. It costs nothing extra but limits what the mover owes to 60 cents per pound per item, so a 10-pound laptop is covered for about $6. Full Value Protection is the default, and you only get the 60-cent option if you sign a waiver.

How long do I have to file a damage claim?

Nine months from delivery to file a written claim with the mover. Note damage on the inventory before you sign at delivery, take photos, and keep every document.

Will my phone number be sold to lots of companies?

No. Your request goes to three movers at most, and the consent box names who can contact you. You can ask us to delete your request at any time; see the Privacy Policy.
